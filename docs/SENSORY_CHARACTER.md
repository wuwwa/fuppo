# Sensory character

Direction from the user, September 18, 2026: give each shape a sensory and emotional character, with the subtlety of tasting notes on coffee. A toy can suggest a texture, scent, place or memory through the way it looks, moves and responds. The profiles below are draft interpretations of that direction, not finalized copy or a claim that those associations have been validated.

**Every shape should feel like something, and remind you of something.** Its character should be apparent in ordinary play. Words offer a small invitation to notice it.

## Designing the character

Begin with a familiar sensation or moment: cool aloe, freshly washed linen, warm clay in the palms. Choose two or three notes that belong together. One should have a clear connection to the material's response; another can suggest a scent, atmosphere or memory. Leave space for the person's own interpretation. Every toy does not need a food reference, a scent or an explicit emotion.

Carry that intention through contact, continued handling and release. Decide how the material first gives, how resistance develops, and what lingers after letting go. Color, translucency, surface finish and optional sound should support the same character. For example, Butter's warmth could come from its matte surface, yielding resistance and slow recovery; Gel Cube's coolness could come from its translucent depth, dense deformation and smooth return. These associations are design hypotheses to evaluate through use.

Scent and temperature words describe associations, not literal capabilities of the screen. An emotional direction is an invitation, not a promised effect. Keep language concrete and restrained; give people room to feel something different.

## Writing and placement

Use a short line in the collection to suggest character. Keep the toy's recognizable name and make gesture instructions direct and useful. Sensory language belongs where someone chooses an experience; play itself should have room to breathe.

Possible collection copy:

- **Butter:** “Soft give and a slow rise. Hints of warm pastry and an unhurried morning.”
- **Gel Cube:** “Cool, dense softness. Like aloe and rain on glass.”
- **Silk:** “A cool sheen, a trailing fold, a little afternoon light.”

Use “hints of” or “like” when helpful, without turning them into a template repeated on every card. Favor a few specific words over a paragraph of mood. A short sensory line can replace the existing description; extra badges, meters and overlays are unnecessary.

## Reviewing the feel

Try the toy before reading its notes. Ask what its response brings to mind, then compare that with the intended character. People do not need to name the same scent or memory. Look for a coherent impression, distinguishable materials and a desire to repeat the gesture. If the words suggest a sensation the interaction cannot support, refine the behavior or change the words.

Preserve the [dependable interaction standard](FIDGET_QUALITY.md): immediate response, satisfying continuation and a complete resolution whenever the person stops. Character should survive brief touches, keyboard use, reduced motion and muted sound. A slow material still acknowledges contact immediately. Use the [touch design principles](INTERACTION_PLAN.md#touch-as-a-source-of-material-feel) to turn the intended sensation into concrete behavior.
